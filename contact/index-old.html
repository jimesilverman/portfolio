March 2022 – Present

ADP

You’ve probably heard of ADP. You might even notice their logo on your paycheck. The company’s been around since the 1950s and has been a staple in the global economy ever since.

I joined ADP in 2022 seeking an opportunity to lead the modernization of their service technology products. Designing internal tools used daily by over 60,000 service representatives, the role enables me to simplify the people’s workdays at a massive scale.

Design leadership in a large organization.

As a Lead UX Designer in a sprawling team, my day-to-day activities vary broadly. At a high-level, I like to say that I’m the one who makes sure we’re solving problems with our users’ needs in mind.

More specifically, I…

  • Collaborate across teams, facilitating the right connections and discussions.
  • Lead meetings, demos, and design-thinking exercises.
  • Deep-dive into research to help teams understand problem spaces.
  • Explore and pitch ideas for the product roadmap.
  • Educate team members how to leverage new tools and processes.
  • Mentor and coach other designers (and sometimes engineers).
  • Demonstrate and quantify the value of “good UX” to leadership.
  • Get my hands dirty with creating user flows, service maps, wireframes, mockups, and other deliverables.

Case Studies

Moving 25,000+ Service Reps to a new platform.

I led the UX Design on one of the largest Salesforce implementations ever, as ADP transitioned their entire customer service organization (all 25,000+ service reps) to the platform. The first step was to create a pilot environment for one of our many business units to serve as a proof of concept that would expand to the larger enterprise.

As with many enterprise tools, users would need to be able to use it to do their job on day one. Before any design could be created, a detailed and thorough discovery period was necessary to identify all necessary functionality and define feature parity with the (soon to be) legacy system.

The team and I interviewed a ton of stakeholders, subject matter experts, and field agents. This enabled us to map out the journey, create common personas, and identify pain points in their current process.

These pain points were especially important. There is inevitably resistance when a large user base is forced into a new system. However, we can target these “moments that matter” then flip them into a satisfying user experience that will speed user acceptance and adoption.

One of these moments are the legacy “case wizards” which are universally used and loathed by every agent we talked to. Each wizard is a basic series of questions regarding the customer’s issue that the agent fills out and sends to another team.

Turning frustration into a win.

I know. It’s just a simple data collection flow, how bad could it be? We found that the pain points were endless from start to finish. Searching for the correct wizard was a multiple-step chore. Wizard questions were riddled with broken form fields and grammatical errors. The wizards pop up in a modal that blocks the relevant information the agent needs to retrieve. But if the modal is closed, the wizard is canceled without saving any work.

Choose one: yes or yes.

To make matters worse, some of these wizards are 20+ screens long with no indication of progress.

With this in mind, I targeted a few of the most used wizards for the pilot release to turn into shiny new “Guided Flows.” These guided flows dramatically improved the user experience by sticking to best practices, pre-populating data when possible, reducing the amount of user input, and automating any changes to the case.

Partnership-driven design.

Once it became time to start creating screens, I quickly realized how limited my design toolbox was. Working inside the constraints of a third-party tool was relatively new to me, especially one as gargantuan and sprawling as Salesforce. Think of the atomic design methodology, but there’s no atoms or molecules–just organisms.

You see the problem?

For this project to be successful within an aggressive deadline, I needed to know what was technically feasible out-of-the-box and what would require custom coding. I started out by familiarizing myself with the product from both a design and development perspective. This helped me create solutions that were easy to implement and enabled smooth communication with development teams.

Communication of page layouts and interaction design was done in a combination of Figma prototypes and actual code within Salesforce, with constant collaboration across teams. We engaged our in-house user research team to help collect feedback throughout the process, not only to refine the design, but also to help identify gaps in our understanding of user needs. This allowed the teams to stay agile while constantly iterating forward.

  • The account page, where agents find information.

  • An example case page, where agents do the work.

Laying the foundation for the enterprise.

Currently, a pilot of the new platform is underway for a small group of users. As I mentioned before, ADP is a huge company and change takes time. It's going very well so far, but this is merely the beginning of a multi-year effort. I'll report back once I have more data.

Launched Mid-2023

Redesigning a critical service tool

I led the redesign of ADP's Client Engagement Hub (CEH) while it was in active daily use by all customer service agents. The transition needed to be seamless, as to not interfere with daily operations – but also delightful, so that users resistant to change wouldn't come at me with pitchforks.

The catalyst for this redesign was the company-wide transition to a new design system. This system provided components and patterns that could be leveraged by all apps within the company to create a consistent experience no matter what you are using.

More details to come.

I'll update this with more details as soon as I can. In the meantime, I'll tell you that the redesign was extremely well received. CEH's monthly NPS score immediately jumped from 27 to 40. That's pretty much unheard of for an internal tool.
